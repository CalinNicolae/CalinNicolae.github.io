About the internship

From 9 February to 13 May 2026 I did my internship at Eniris, an energy management company behind SmartgridOne, a platform that manages distributed energy resources like batteries, solar inverters, EV chargers, and heat pumps. Eniris builds its product around being vendor-independent, so the platform relies on how well it talks to hardware from hundreds of different manufacturers. My internship ran on two tracks at once: writing Python integrations for those devices, and working with the newly formed cybersecurity team on the company's path toward NIS2 and ISO 27001 compliance.

Building device integrations

Most of my days went into device integrations. Each one is a Python module that runs on the SmartgridOne Controller and bridges a specific device, usually over Modbus, to the Eniris platform. I worked on seven in total. The deepest was the Fairland InverX Elite, a hybrid solar inverter that took about three weeks to get right. Most of that time was register-map debugging, where the values the device returned did not match what the driver expected. The only reliable way to find out why was to read the registers straight off the device with pymodbus and compare them line by line. The fix I am most proud of there was a slow-communication problem, where each read cycle was taking 32 to 34 seconds. Tracking it down meant understanding the polling loop, the framework's scheduling, and a Docker container that was holding the device connection at the same time. Once that was sorted, the cycle dropped to 3 to 4 seconds. The Sunsynk integration taught the opposite lesson: a full delivery cycle from code, through review, to coordinating with a real client and deploying against their hardware.

The security side: a SIEM proof of concept

The cybersecurity half is where my specialisation came in. The team's biggest gap was that Eniris had no centralised logging or monitoring, so if something happened on the production systems, nobody would see it. When the first instinct was to jump straight to picking a SIEM, I argued for building a digital asset inventory first, because you cannot scope a SIEM for assets you have not mapped yet. The team agreed, and that inventory became the first deliverable.

From there I built a proof of concept around one question: can the Elastic Stack be used as a SIEM solution? An Elastic Agent on a test controller shipped logs over TLS to Elasticsearch and Kibana on a Hetzner server. The interesting part was the network path. Two obvious options were rejected for solid security reasons, so the working design put an Nginx reverse proxy in front of Elasticsearch on port 443, which the firewall already allowed. Until that proxy was in place the agent could reach the network but no logs arrived. After that, the logs started flowing. My answer to the question was yes, with conditions: the free tier gets you started but not finished, the network integration has to be designed up front, and a SIEM is only as good as the detection rules you tune for your own environment.

Other work, and what I took away

Around those two tracks I picked up smaller jobs: reworking how controller VPN addresses were stored, building an HTTP proxy through an FRP tunnel, running a network-layer penetration test against test controllers, and fixing live documentation and front-end bugs. I also recorded an IT podcast with Angelos, a developer on the team, which is on this site as its own post.

The biggest growth for me was practical. Docker and Linux became my default working environment, I got real hands-on time with the Elastic Stack and with reverse proxies, and I learned where my habit of solving everything myself stops being useful and asking is the better move. The clearest professional lesson was that speaking up with a clear argument can change a project's direction, and the asset-inventory call was the proof.
