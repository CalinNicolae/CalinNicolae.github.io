About the episode

The Howest Student IT Podcast episode on Claude, AI and their security considerations brought together two computer science students and a professional software engineer to discuss the real-world implications of AI tools in modern development. This wasn't a surface-level overview, it was a serious conversation about how AI is changing the landscape of software engineering, the risks that come with it, and what it means for developers entering the field today.

What was covered

The episode explored several critical themes. First, we discussed how AI has fundamentally changed what it means to be a software engineer in 2026. Angelos shared that AI can accelerate work significantly, especially for tasks like writing drivers or setting up boilerplate code, but it requires critical thinking. He noted that AI hallucinates and that the quality of your prompts directly determines the quality of your output.

We then moved into the conversation around junior developers versus senior developers. The idea that a junior with AI skills might match a senior was challenged. Angelos made a compelling point: while AI can help juniors move faster, it increases the barrier to entry because there's more competition, and juniors need to understand architectural patterns and system restrictions, things that only come with experience.

A significant portion of the episode focused on security risks. We discussed the real danger of uploading API keys, credentials, and sensitive data into AI systems. Angelos was clear: people don't think about these risks enough, and it's a major backdoor. This became even more relevant when we brought up that Claude's source code had been leaked, raising questions about how we should trust tools we're feeding sensitive information into .

We also covered the difference between using AI and traditional Google searches, the value of tools like Claude Code, Model Context Protocols (MCPs), and autonomous AI agents. Angelos was cautious about full automation in production environments, advocating for a "human last call" approach.

The conversation concluded with thoughts on future trends: energy costs and their impact on AI model pricing, potential AI bubbles, the importance of prompt security and prompt injection attacks, and the evolving user interface for AI tools.

My takeaway

This episode reinforced something I've been thinking about throughout my cybersecurity studies: AI is not a neutral tool. It's powerful, but power requires responsibility. The most striking part for me was the security angle; how easy it is for developers (and companies) to accidentally compromise their systems by sharing sensitive information with AI tools without considering the consequences.

Angelos's perspective as a working engineer was invaluable. He didn't treat AI as a savior or dismiss it as hype. Instead, he presented a balanced, pragmatic view: use it where it adds value, be skeptical of its output, understand the risks, and always maintain critical oversight, especially in production environments.

The point about junior developers resonated with me too. It's clear that AI won't replace the need for foundational knowledge and experience. If anything, it's raising the bar. To be competitive, you need to understand not just how to use AI, but how to use it wisely.

You still need to know what you're doing. AI accelerates work, but it doesn't replace understanding.

My verdict

I consider this podcast episode very relevant and valuable. Unlike many AI discussions that veer into either enthusiasm or unfounded fear, this conversation was grounded in real experience and practical considerations. Angelos brought depth to every question, and the mix of student perspectives with professional insights made the information accessible and educational.

I would recommend this episode to any developer, especially those just starting out, who wants a clear look at how AI fits into professional work. The security discussion alone is worth the listen.
